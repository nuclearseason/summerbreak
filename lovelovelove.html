Notes on the translation

A song like Love Love Love struggles in translation with the push and pull between meaning and feeling. A translation more accurate in literal meaning is often less accurate stylistically and emotionally. Arguably the latter two are more important in artistic cases, but I also have to pay respect to the deliberateness with which the lyricists chose their words.

The verses of the song that make it so fun to listen to, that made me want to translate it, rely on a rhyme scheme that is just the same word ending over and over again: “적,” meaning something like “a time when” or “instance.” Both sets of verses are a series of questions and meandering responses starting with the first question, “있나요?” which is kind of like, “Do you have it?” or “Has it happened?” and the first response, “있겠죠,” or “You probably do”; “It probably has.” Every sentence (or not sentence, grammatically, but I’m not remembering what it’s called and you know what I mean) following ends with “적.” So in the most accurate sense the first verse starts like, “Do you have it? A time when you loved? A time when you fell at first sight like in a movie? A time when you stayed up all night holding the phone?” And in the most accurate sense the following verse starts like, “You probably do, a time when you loved, a time when you went broke because of an anniversary, a time when you did things right but said sorry…”

But because of the difference in word order within a sentence in Kor/Eng, the verb placement and all that, the repetition ends up moving to the front when translated into English. That usually doesn’t make for a very memorable rhyme, and it totally loses the cadence of the song. It’s impossible to replicate the rhythm of the repeated 적, this blunt -ck sound, but the ends of each line at least have to rhyme to even remotely resemble it. Also, “a time when” is a more bumbling utterance than the monosyllabic “적.” Repeated in every single line, it gets old fast.

There are a lot of different translation rhyme patterns I could have chosen, but I just went with AABB and stuck with it. It meant I had to creatively interpret some lines, but none of them stray far from its original form. After all, there are many ways to say something. For example, I chose to translate what really is “went broke because of an anniversary” as “splurged on dates like you were rich enough,” which omits the detail of “anniversary” and does change the overall nuance. But they convey the same idea.

In the chorus, the third line is really “though it hurts, it keeps repeating,” which sounds more unnatural. A more vernacular way to say it might be, “it hurts but keeps happening” or “it hurts but doesn’t stop.” I went with “It just repeats, I don’t heal.” Similar minor liberties were taken with other lines. I also, for the sake of rhythm, added some words that weren’t originally there but did not necessarily change the meaning of the line. “Waited alone in the pouring rain” in verse one is actually “waited in the pouring rain” or “waited as the rain poured down,” but it’s already implied that the waiting-person is alone. The “alone” helps add a needed beat to sync up the translated line better with the song’s actual movement, even if it’s not perfect.

We don’t really have pronouns the way English has them. A lot of lines in Korean songs do not necessarily have pronouns. I’ve found that this phenomenon enshrouds Korean lyrics and poetry in an ambiguity that makes it very exciting to interpret: who did what? who’s talking to who? It’s easy to attach double meanings to every line. The same is true for this song, which is already a little ambiguous in its female-male vocal dialogue—in the heteronormative sense that plagues commercial stories. Are they the ex-lovers, or are they just comrades in heartbreak?

A heteronormative translation is made possible here by the explicit femininity in the third verse, which addresses the listener’s “makeup” and “long (straight) hair,” and would lead most people to see the ex-lover as “him.” But I don’t believe in a heteronormative translation so when I had to write in a pronoun as necessary for sentence structure I chose the trusty “they.” At any rate, it’s never clear if the listener, the lover-turned-griever, is supposed to be a specific kind of person, or the same person throughout the whole song. Maybe Epik High is speaking to just whoever will listen.

The line I’m least satisfied with is the last one of verse 2. “가는 뒷모습을 지켜본 적.” 뒷모습 is extremely hard to translate. The closest thing I can think of is “backside,” but that word also can refer to someone’s butt, and that kind of ruins everything. It’s closest to “retreating figure,” but that’s not the same thing as the detail of the back. Because what 뒷모습 gets at is the forlornness of having someone’s back turned to you, getting smaller and smaller as they move forwards while leaving you, standing in the same spot, behind. And it’s not even really the specific person that’s important, but the back on its own, and all the physical and emotional distance that brings. Just as weighty in its implication is the simultaneous secretive intimacy, because you are watching this person, and they can’t stop you or actually even know you’re watching them (unless they’re holding a mirror or some other mood-killer). You are seeing a part of their physical self that they have never and will never truly see themselves—not in the way another observer can.

Plus, the word 지켜본 (지켜보다) isn’t just “watch.” There’s more care to it. It’s kind of like “watch over,” kind of protective. It’s slightly more selfless and a lot more pathetic than just “watching” someone leave you. You are a helpless guardian, too attached to the person who broke your heart. You are unable to not care.

This all being said, the most literal way I can translate it is, “the time you watched the back turned to you, leaving.” But since that’s a crunchy set of words I was thinking more like “watched a retreating back at the end” or “watched the back turned to you as it went.” I ended up going with the second.
